01 — Overview

The challenge.

How do you tell the story of 70+ years of Formula 1 — combining performance data, safety regulations, and accident history — while keeping the experience both comprehensible and engaging for any audience?

This was the core challenge of the VisualDon course project at HEIG-VD. The goal was to transform complex, multi-dimensional historical data into a coherent visual narrative — one that guides the user through time, reveals patterns, and creates emotion without overwhelming them.

02 — Data

The data behind the story.

The project required sourcing, cleaning, and correlating several independent datasets to build a coherent timeline of F1 history. The complexity of the data work was a key part of the challenge.

03 — UX & Storytelling

Designing the narrative.

Data Analysis → Information Architecture → Wireframes → Scrollytelling Structure → UI Design → Development

Information architecture

We structured the experience as a chronological journey — each decade of F1 history becomes a chapter. The user is guided progressively through time without needing to navigate: the scroll is the story.

Scrollytelling

Scrollytelling was chosen to give the user full control of the pace of discovery. Each scroll triggers a new data reveal, transition, or 3D animation — creating a sense of forward motion through history.

06 — 3D & Interactive Experience

The car that evolves.

A central element of the experience is a 3D Formula 1 car — rendered in WebGL via Three.js — that visually transforms as the user scrolls through the decades. As regulations changed and safety innovations were introduced, the car's geometry, aeroparts, and cockpit evolve in real-time.

This 3D object serves as both a navigation anchor and a storytelling device — making abstract regulatory data tangible and visual.

09 — Reflections

What I learned.

This project pushed me to think at the intersection of data, design, and code in ways I had not before. Transforming raw CSV datasets into a visual, emotional experience taught me that data visualization is as much an editorial discipline as a technical one.

Working with D3.js and Three.js simultaneously — and making them cooperate with GSAP scroll triggers — gave me a much deeper understanding of the browser's rendering model and performance constraints.

Beyond the technical side, I learned how powerful storytelling structure is in data work. The order in which information is revealed, the pace of transitions, and the choice of what to show vs. what to hide are all UX decisions that directly affect comprehension.

Working as a team on a complex, technically diverse project also taught me how important shared design systems and component agreements are from day one.
